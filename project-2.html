Fully Analogue Component Identifier Circuit

← All projects

Summary

Designed and built a fully analogue component identifier on a single breadboard that distinguishes between an open circuit, resistors, capacitors and inductors, and indicates the result with three LEDs. It uses no microcontroller, only a 555 timer, comparators and transistors, and was completed as a two-person project for ELEC3106 with myself as the primary designer.

  • Ltspice
  • Analogue design
  • 555 timer
  • Oscilloscope Testing

What it does

  • Detects an open circuit, resistors (~510 Ω to 49 kΩ), capacitors (~150 pF to 50 nF) and inductors (~5 mH to 20 mH).
  • Exceeds the project brief’s required ranges and covers the extension ranges and inductor identification.
  • Draws a maximum of 91 mW (measured experimentally).
  • Total component cost of $7.50 (excluding the breadboard).

    • How it works

  • A 555 timer generates a 1 kHz, 0 to 5 V square wave, which is applied to the unknown component in series with a 47 kΩ resistor and a 1 kΩ pull-down.
  • Each component type produces a distinct voltage response: resistors form a voltage divider, capacitors act as a low-pass filter, and at this frequency inductors behave as a near short circuit.

  • LM339N comparators with fixed reference voltages (2.5 V, 49.5 mV and 125 mV) turn those voltage levels into digital decisions.

  • Peak detectors (diode and capacitor) hold the brief comparator outputs steady, and BJT common-emitter stages drive the LEDs at a safe current.

  • Three LEDs encode the result: COMPONENT (any component present), VOLTAGE_DIV (capacitor or open circuit) and INDUCTOR.

    • Design and Verification

  • Selected component values by calculation, for example the RC time constant for the capacitor range and the peak-detector timing.

  • Simulated the full circuit in LTspice, then built it and tested every category experimentally with an oscilloscope.

  • Experimental results matched the simulated bounds and extended them. The upper capacitance limit went from about 50 nF in simulation to about 3.3 µF in practice, limited only by response time (the LED takes about a second to turn on).

  • Explained the differences using resistor tolerances, the 555’s non-ideal duty cycle, and comparator offsets and bias currents.

    • Outcome

    Skills gained: analogue circuit design, LTspice simulation, comparator and transistor circuits, breadboard prototyping, oscilloscope testing and teamwork/communication skills
